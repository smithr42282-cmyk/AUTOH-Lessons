SYSTEM OVERVIEW

What Is ETCS?

On older vehicles, a physical cable connected the accelerator pedal directly to the throttle plate. ETCS removes that cable. Instead, the driver's pedal input becomes an electrical signal, and the ECM/PCM decides how far to open the throttle plate — this is why the system is often called "drive-by-wire."

Why it matters to a technician Because there is no mechanical link between the pedal and the throttle plate, you cannot diagnose a "no throttle response" complaint by inspecting a cable. Every part of the system — input, decision, and output — is electronic, so the scan tool and wiring diagrams become your primary diagnostic tools.

Removing the cable also lets the ECM/PCM take control of throttle angle for reasons the driver never asked for, including idle speed control, traction/VSA intervention, cruise control, and cylinder deactivation transitions.

KEY COMPONENTS — PART 1

Reading the Driver's Intent: The APP Sensor

The Accelerator Pedal Position (APP) sensor is mounted on the pedal assembly and reports how far the pedal has been pressed.

APP sensor component and function
ComponentFunction
APP Sensor 1 & APP Sensor 2Two potentiometer-type (or Hall-effect) sensors housed in one assembly, each producing an independent voltage signal proportional to pedal travel.
Why two sensors? The ECM/PCM continuously compares APP1 and APP2. Because their voltage curves are offset (for example, one might range roughly 0.5–4.5V while the other ranges roughly 1.7–3.0V across the same pedal travel), the ECM/PCM can verify the two signals correlate correctly. This redundancy is the foundation of the system's fail-safe design, which you'll cover in a later slide.

KEY COMPONENTS — PART 2

Moving the Throttle Plate: TP Sensor & Throttle Actuator

Throttle actuator and TP sensor component and function
ComponentFunction
Throttle Actuator (Motor)A small DC motor built into the throttle body. The ECM/PCM controls its direction and speed (via a motor driver circuit, often PWM) to open or close the throttle plate.
Throttle Position (TP) Sensor 1 & 2Two sensors that report the actual angle of the throttle plate back to the ECM/PCM, again for redundancy and cross-checking.
Return SpringA mechanical spring inside the throttle body that pulls the plate toward a fixed, partially-open default angle any time the motor is not energized.

Keep that return spring in mind — it is the mechanical foundation of the fail-safe strategy you'll see in a few slides.

The desired angle is not simply "pedal percentage = throttle percentage." The ECM/PCM blends the driver's pedal request with other inputs before commanding the actuator, including:

  • Engine speed and load, coolant temperature (cold-idle-up)
  • PCM-requested idle speed control
  • Cruise control, VSA/TCS traction intervention
  • Transmission shift quality (brief throttle "blips" during shifts, on applicable models)
  • Cylinder deactivation transitions, on VCM-equipped engines

The TP sensor feedback closes the loop: the ECM/PCM keeps adjusting motor current until actual angle matches desired angle, then holds it there.

KNOWLEDGE CHECK 1

Control Loop Check

1. Which two sensor signals does the ECM/PCM compare to detect a pedal-sensor fault?
APP1 and APP2 are the two accelerator pedal position sensors. The ECM/PCM cross-checks their offset voltage curves against each other to confirm the driver's pedal input is being read correctly.
2. What provides the ECM/PCM with feedback confirming the throttle plate actually reached the commanded angle?
The TP (Throttle Position) sensors report actual plate angle back to the ECM/PCM, closing the control loop.

FAIL-SAFE STRATEGIES — PART 1

Detecting a Fault: Sensor Monitoring & Cross-Checks

Because ETCS has no mechanical backup, Honda/Acura designs multiple layers of self-checking into the system. The ECM/PCM continuously monitors for:

  • APP1 vs. APP2 disagreement — the two pedal signals no longer correlate within an expected range.
  • TP1 vs. TP2 disagreement — the two throttle position signals no longer correlate.
  • Commanded angle vs. actual angle disagreement — the motor is told to move the plate to a position, but the TP sensors show it didn't get there (mechanical binding, motor fault, wiring fault).
  • Circuit-level faults — open or shorted sensor/actuator circuits, out-of-range voltages, or loss of a reference voltage.
  • Throttle actuator control (TAC) module or relay faults — loss of the ability to energize the throttle motor at all.
Key idea Redundancy exists so the ECM/PCM never has to "trust" a single sensor with something as safety-critical as engine power output. Any disagreement between the redundant signals is treated as a potential fault.

FAIL-SAFE STRATEGIES — PART 2

Responding to a Fault: Limp-Home & Shutdown Modes

When the ECM/PCM confirms an ETCS fault, it responds with a strategy matched to the severity of the fault:

Fault severity and ECM/PCM response
Fault SeverityECM/PCM Response
Minor disagreement / single non-critical DTCMIL illuminated, DTC stored, engine may continue running with limited throttle authority (reduced maximum angle) and/or a fixed elevated idle.
Loss of reliable APP or TP dataECM/PCM commands a fixed, limited "default" throttle angle just enough to allow slow, engine-braked driving to a safe location — commonly called limp-home mode. Full acceleration is not available.
Severe/undiagnosable fault, or TAC relay commanded openThe throttle actuator control relay is de-energized, cutting all power to the throttle motor. With the motor un-powered, the return spring pulls the plate to its mechanical default position, and the engine can idle or run at low power only.
Safety note The spring-return default position is a fixed mechanical angle, not "closed" and not "wide open." It is designed to allow just enough airflow to limp the vehicle safely, never to accelerate normally.

In every case, the ECM/PCM also stores a Diagnostic Trouble Code (DTC) and illuminates the Malfunction Indicator Lamp (MIL) so the fault is recorded for you to retrieve.

KNOWLEDGE CHECK 2

Fail-Safe Check

3. If the ECM/PCM de-energizes the throttle actuator motor entirely, what brings the throttle plate to its default position?
With the motor unpowered, the mechanical return spring inside the throttle body pulls the plate to a fixed default angle that allows limited, limp-home operation only.
4. What is the purpose of "limp-home" throttle strategy?
Limp-home mode restricts throttle authority so the vehicle can still be driven, cautiously, to a safe stopping point — it is not meant for normal driving.

SERVICING PROCEDURES

Servicing an ETCS Throttle Body

  • Verify the complaint and pull DTCs first. Never disconnect or replace an ETCS component before confirming, with the scan tool, what fault is actually stored.
  • Follow the model-specific flowchart in Service Express for the exact DTC retrieved — ETCS troubleshooting steps are not identical across all models and years.
  • Throttle body cleaning: use only a cleaner labeled safe for oxygen sensors/throttle bodies; avoid spraying solvent directly into the throttle actuator motor or TP sensor connector, and never manually force the throttle plate open by hand against the return spring beyond the manufacturer's procedure.
  • After replacing or disconnecting the throttle body, ECM/PCM, or battery, a throttle body/idle relearn (reset) procedure is typically required so the ECM/PCM can re-learn the closed-throttle reference position. Follow the exact steps and conditions listed in Service Express for the specific model — sequence and timing matter.
  • Clear codes and verify the repair by retrieving live APP/TP data and confirming commanded angle matches actual angle through the full pedal range, then road test to confirm the MIL stays off.
Safety precaution Because the throttle plate is motor-driven, keep hands and tools clear of the throttle bore whenever the ignition is on or the 12V system is powered — the ECM/PCM can move the plate without warning during self-tests, relearn procedures, or bi-directional scan tool commands.
