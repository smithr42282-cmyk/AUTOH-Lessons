Discussion: The Physics Behind Engine Design
Discussion Topic • Honda PACT Competency: Engine Mechanical / Combustion Fundamentals
Why this matters: Air pressure, temperature, volume, displacement, and compression ratio are not five separate facts to memorize — they are one connected system. Every compression test, knock complaint, and boost-pressure fault you diagnose traces back to how these five ideas interact inside a cylinder.

The 5 concepts

1. Air pressure
Air pressure is the force air exerts on its surroundings, caused by air molecules in motion. In an engine, atmospheric pressure is what pushes the air-fuel charge into the cylinder on the intake stroke, and it's the baseline every gauge reading (vacuum, boost, compression) is measured against.
2. Pressure and temperature
When a gas is held in a fixed space and heated, its pressure rises — the molecules move faster and strike the cylinder walls harder. This is why cylinder pressure climbs sharply once combustion begins, and why an overheated engine or a hot intake charge is more prone to detonation.
3. Pressure and volume
When a gas is compressed into a smaller volume, its pressure rises — the same number of molecules are packed into a tighter space, so they collide more often. This is the mechanical heart of the compression stroke: shrinking cylinder volume from BDC to TDC is what builds pressure before the spark ever fires.
4. Displacement
Displacement is the total volume swept by all pistons as they travel from BDC to TDC — it's the "size" of the engine (e.g., the Odyssey's J35Y6 displaces 3.5 liters). Displacement, combined with cylinder count and configuration, sets the upper limit on how much air-fuel mixture the engine can process, which is the starting point for how much power it can produce.
5. Compression ratio
Compression ratio (CR) is the ratio of cylinder volume at BDC to cylinder volume at TDC. It ties concepts 2, 3, and 4 together: a higher CR squeezes the same displaced volume into a smaller space at TDC, driving both pressure and temperature higher. That improves thermal efficiency and torque, but also raises knock risk — which is why naturally aspirated and turbocharged engines are tuned to very different compression ratios.
