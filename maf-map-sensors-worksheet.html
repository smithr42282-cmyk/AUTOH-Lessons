Part A — Reading: MAF & MAP Sensors

What the MAF sensor does

The Mass Air Flow (MAF) sensor measures the mass of air entering the engine. It sits in the intake duct between the air filter and the throttle body. The ECM uses that measurement to calculate how much fuel to inject for the correct air-fuel mixture. The sensor has its own electronic circuit that senses airflow and converts it into a signal the ECM can read, usually a voltage.

Types of heated MAF sensors

Two heated designs are used today:

  • Hot wire: a very fine wire, usually platinum, is stretched across the air stream. The sensor's circuit keeps the wire at a set temperature above the intake air (constant temperature). As air flows past, it carries heat away, so the circuit must send more current to keep the wire hot. That heating current is converted into the output signal. A second, unheated element measures the air temperature so the circuit always knows what "above intake air" means. Some older hot-wire sensors briefly heat the wire after shutdown to burn off dirt.
  • Hot film: instead of a wire, thin-film resistors are printed on a small chip or ceramic plate. A heater sits between temperature sensors placed upstream and downstream. By comparing them, the sensor measures air mass and can also sense reverse flow and pulsation in the intake. Hot-film sensors are tougher and more accurate, and they are the most common type today.

Most MAF sensors, including most Honda units, also have the Intake Air Temperature (IAT) sensor built into the same housing. It's a separate sensor with its own signal wire. It tells the ECM the air temperature, because cold air is denser than warm air.

The resistance principle

The sensing element is made from a metal with a high positive temperature coefficient of resistance, usually platinum (some designs use tungsten or platinum alloys). Its resistance rises when it heats up and falls when it cools, and even a small temperature change gives a measurable resistance change.

More airflow→Element cools→Resistance drops→Circuit adds current→Signal rises→ECM adds fuel

Less airflow reverses the chain: less cooling, less current needed, lower signal. The circuit is always working to hold the element at the same temperature.

Signal types

  • Analog voltage: rises with airflow (most Honda MAF sensors). On the scope it's a DC analog signal.
  • Frequency: pulses get faster as airflow rises (some other makes). On the scope it's a digital signal.
  • The HDS data list shows MAF in grams per second (g/s) after the ECM converts the signal.

Older MAF designs

  • Vane (flap) meter: incoming air pushes a spring-loaded door connected to a potentiometer.
  • Karman vortex: a strut in the air stream sheds small swirls (vortices); the sensor counts them, so its output is a frequency.

These were replaced by heated sensors, which are smaller, have no moving parts, restrict airflow less and measure air mass directly.

The MAP sensor

The Manifold Absolute Pressure (MAP) sensor measures the pressure inside the intake manifold, after the throttle plate. "Absolute" means it's measured against a perfect vacuum (0 kPa). It's a 3-wire sensor: 5 V reference, signal and sensor ground.

  • Key on, engine off: the manifold is at outside air pressure, so MAP equals barometric pressure (about 101 kPa near sea level) and the signal voltage is high.
  • Warm idle: the throttle is nearly closed, so manifold vacuum is high, absolute pressure is low, and the signal voltage is low.
  • Wide open throttle: the manifold fills back toward barometric pressure, so the voltage is high again.
  • Vacuum = barometric pressure − manifold absolute pressure.

From MAP, engine RPM and air temperature, the ECM can calculate airflow. This is called speed density.

Some engines use only a MAP sensor, some only a MAF, and many use both. With both, the ECM can cross-check them, detect air leaks, and handle boost on turbocharged or supercharged engines.

Common mix-ups to avoid: a hot-wire MAF works at a constant temperature, not a constant voltage. "Hot film" is a thin-film chip, not a "dual wire" sensor. The IAT is a separate built-in sensor, not a redundant one. The ECM receives the sensor's finished signal, not the heating current.
